1. What we're actually simulating

Atmospheric heat shimmer (the wavy distortion you see above hot pavement, jet exhaust, or distant scenery on a hot day) is an optical refraction phenomenon. The atmosphere is a stack of air parcels at slightly different temperatures and densities. Refractive index $n$ varies with density:

\[ n(\mathbf{r}, t) - 1 \;\propto\; \rho(\mathbf{r}, t) \;\propto\; \frac{P}{T(\mathbf{r}, t)} \]

where $P$ is pressure (≈ constant), $T$ is local temperature, and $\rho$ is air density. Hot air is less dense, so $n$ drops. Light passing through these inhomogeneities accumulates a small, position-dependent phase delay; the resulting wavefront is no longer planar, so when we project the image onto the focal plane each pixel appears to come from a slightly displaced location.

If the atmosphere were static this would just be a fixed geometric distortion — a constant lens. What makes it shimmer is that the refractive-index field is turbulent and time-varying, and crucially, statistically self-similar across a wide range of scales. That self-similarity is the property we exploit.

What this project is not doing. We are not solving the eikonal equation, ray-tracing through a 3D refractive medium, or computing a wave-optics propagation integral. We approximate the cumulative effect of refraction along the optical path as a single 2D image warp $(x, y) \to (x + \delta x,\, y + \delta y)$ whose displacement field $(\delta x, \delta y)$ has the correct statistical signature.

2. Kolmogorov and the Von Karman PSD

Kolmogorov's 1941 theory of homogeneous isotropic turbulence (K41) predicts that, in the inertial subrange (between the energy-injection scale and the viscous dissipation scale), the energy spectrum follows

\[ E(k) \;=\; C\, \varepsilon^{2/3}\, k^{-5/3} \]

where $k = |\mathbf{k}|$ is the spatial wavenumber and $\varepsilon$ is the energy dissipation rate. This is the celebrated $-5/3$ law. For atmospheric optics what matters is not the velocity spectrum but the refractive-index structure function, which inherits the same scaling, leading to a 2D phase power spectral density

\[ \Phi_\phi(k) \;=\; 0.023\, r_0^{-5/3}\, k^{-11/3} \]

(The $-11/3$ rather than $-5/3$ comes from the fact that we are looking at the spectrum of integrated phase along a ray, and at the radial distribution of 2D power.) The constant $r_0$ is the Fried parameter — the diameter over which the rms wavefront error reaches one radian. Smaller $r_0$ ⇒ stronger turbulence.

Pure $k^{-11/3}$ diverges at $k \to 0$ (energy-injection scale → infinity), so we use the Von Karman form which adds a finite outer scale $L_0$:

\[ \boxed{\;\Phi_\phi(k) \;=\; 0.023\, r_0^{-5/3}\, \left(k^2 + \tfrac{1}{L_0^2}\right)^{-11/6}\;} \]

Below $k \sim 1/L_0$ the spectrum flattens into a plateau; above it the spectrum follows the inertial-range $-11/3$ slope. This single equation is the engine behind everything that follows.

3. Pipeline overview

4. Generating a phase screen — FFT-filtered noise

The recipe to produce a 2D random field with prescribed power spectrum $\Phi(k)$ is a textbook trick:

  1. Generate complex white Gaussian noise $W(\mathbf{k})$ — independent real and imaginary parts, unit variance, on the same FFT grid as the desired output.
  2. Multiply by $\sqrt{\Phi_\phi(\mathbf{k})}$ pointwise. This colors the noise so its periodogram matches the target PSD.
  3. Inverse FFT, take the real part. The result is a real-valued, spatially correlated random field with the desired statistics.
\[ \phi(\mathbf{r}) \;=\; \mathrm{Re}\Big\{\,\mathcal{F}^{-1}\big[\,W(\mathbf{k})\, \sqrt{\Phi_\phi(\mathbf{k})}\,\big]\,\Big\} \]

Concretely (lifted from kolmogorov_screen_fft):

fy, fx = np.fft.fftfreq(H, ps)[:,None], np.fft.fftfreq(W, ps)[None,:]
k2 = (2π·fx)² + (2π·fy)²
psd = 0.023 · r0^(-5/3) · (k2 + 1/L0²)^(-11/6)
psd[0,0] = 0          # zero-mean field
noise = randn(H,W) + 1j·randn(H,W)
field = ifft2( noise · sqrt(psd) ) · const
return field.real

The ps (pixel scale, m/px) is critical: it is what ties the physical Von Karman parameters $r_0, L_0$ to the pixel grid. The eddy size in pixels is approximately $L_0 / \mathrm{ps}$. We exploit this aggressively when picking layer parameters.

Key tuning constraint. If $L_0 / \mathrm{ps} \gtrsim$ image extent, the entire visible region sits in the PSD's flat low-$k$ plateau, and the result is a single huge eddy bulging across the screen. We learned this the hard way during the investigation. Keep all layers with eddy size in pixels $\ll W_\mathrm{img}$.

Why not aotools.PhaseScreenKolmogorov?

The aotools library implements an infinite phase screen via a Cholesky-factored autoregressive process: generate one column at a time, conditional on a window of previous columns. Beautiful for adaptive-optics simulations, but the constructor's matrix factorization is $\mathcal{O}(n^3)$. At our 2048×2048 working size it took 23 seconds; at 4096 it would have been minutes. The pure-FFT approach above generates a 4096×720 screen in ~120 ms because all the heavy lifting is one inverse FFT.

5. Multi-layer atmosphere

Real atmospheric turbulence isn't a single homogeneous medium. Distinct layers — boundary layer, free atmosphere, jet stream, etc. — sit at different altitudes, each with its own effective $r_0$, outer scale $L_0$, and wind vector. They contribute additively to the total integrated phase along the optical path:

\[ \phi_\mathrm{total}(\mathbf{r}, t) \;=\; \sum_{\ell=1}^{N} w_\ell\, \phi_\ell\!\left(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{v}_\ell\, t\right) \]

where $\mathbf{v}_\ell$ is the layer's wind vector and $w_\ell$ is a relative weight (we just normalise each layer's screen to unit standard deviation, then scale by $w_\ell$, so the weights act like dimensionless mix coefficients).

In scene_config.toml the three layers are:

Layer$r_0$ (m)$L_0$ (m)ps (m/px)eddy sizeweightwind (px/s)
1 (slow, mid-scale)0.102.50.55 px0.6(35, 4)

The layers' winds blow in different directions, so the summed field has no preferred propagation direction — exactly what kills the "wave-front giveaway" the old sine implementation had.

6. Frozen-flow animation (Taylor's hypothesis)

Generating a fresh phase screen per frame is expensive (~120 ms each, for three layers × two screens — too slow). Instead we use Taylor's frozen-turbulence hypothesis: turbulent eddies are advected by the mean wind faster than they evolve internally, so

\[ \phi(\mathbf{r}, t) \;\approx\; \phi(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{v}\, t,\; 0) \]

over time intervals comparable to one eddy turnover. We exploit this by generating one wide phase screen per layer (4096 × 720 in our config) at startup, then sliding a 1280-pixel window across it at each frame:

Cost per frame, per layer: one numpy fancy-index slice plus four-tap bilinear interpolation between adjacent rows and columns of the screen — a few milliseconds total. The screen is built once.

7. Depth-aware distortion scaling

A pixel imaging the foreground (e.g. a road 4 km from the camera) sees a much shorter atmospheric path than a pixel imaging the horizon (~20 km). Cumulative phase variance scales with path length, so distortion magnitude should scale with depth.

For each screen pixel we cast a ray $\mathbf{r}(\tau) = \mathbf{c} + \tau\, \mathbf{d}$ from camera position $\mathbf{c}$ in direction $\mathbf{d}$ (computed from the inverse view & projection matrices) and intersect with the ground plane $z = 0$:

\[ \tau^* \;=\; -\frac{c_z}{d_z}\quad\text{(if $d_z<0$, otherwise sky)} \]

The atmospheric path length to the ground hit is $\tau^*$. We clamp to max_distance_m (default 20 km — beyond this the existing atmospheric haze pass already fades the scene to nothing, so heat shimmer should saturate there) and form

\[ s(x,y) \;=\; \left(\frac{\min(\tau^*, d_\max)}{d_\max}\right)^p \quad\in[0,1] \]

with exponent $p$ (default 1.5) controlling the falloff curve. The final per-pixel displacement is

\[ \big(\delta x, \delta y\big) \;=\; A \cdot s(x,y) \cdot \sum_\ell \phi_\ell(\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{v}_\ell t) \]

where $A$ is the amplitude_px knob (default 0.5 px at the horizon).

8. Image warping — PyTorch grid_sample on Apple MPS

Given the per-pixel displacement field $(\delta x, \delta y)$, we warp the base image $I$ by

\[ I_\mathrm{out}(x, y) \;=\; I\!\left(x + \delta x(x,y),\; y + \delta y(x,y)\right) \]

using bilinear interpolation. The base frame is uploaded once to the GPU when the camera moves; only the displacement field crosses the host-device boundary each frame. Apple's MPS backend hits ~150 fps end-to-end at 1280×720, with the warp itself taking ~1.2 ms.

Why bilinear and not nearest neighbour? With sub-pixel displacements (we live in the 0.1–1 px range most of the time), nearest-neighbour rounding causes visible step artifacts on diagonal edges. Bilinear smooths these out and the speed cost on MPS is negligible.

9. Shortcuts & physical compromises

Several things are deliberately wrong in service of a tractable real-time pipeline. None of these matter for an artistic still or short loop, but it's worth being explicit about them.

  • 2D phase screen, not 3D ray integration. A correct treatment would integrate $\int n(\mathbf{r}, t)\,d\ell$ along each ray through a 3D refractive-index field. We replace this with a single 2D phase screen per layer placed at infinity. For ground-level shimmer at km-range scenes this is a good approximation; for near-field effects (e.g. heat above visible exhaust) it would be wrong.
  • Phase ⇒ image displacement, directly. Strictly the wavefront phase $\phi$ relates to apparent angular displacement via the wavefront gradient: $\delta\theta = \lambda/(2\pi)\, \nabla\phi$. We skip the gradient and use the phase screen itself as the displacement field, then renormalise to the pixel-amplitude scale we want. The visual result is statistically indistinguishable for our purposes; only the absolute scale physical interpretation is lost (and our amplitude knob is empirical anyway).
  • Two independent phase screens for $\delta x$ and $\delta y$. Real wavefront tilt is a vector field with rotational/divergence structure ($\nabla\phi$). We instead use two independent Kolmogorov fields, one per axis. This drops a small amount of cross-correlation but keeps the math trivial.
  • Frozen flow ignores eddy lifetime. Turbulent eddies do evolve internally, not just advect. Over a 51-second loop you'd notice no evolution, only translation. Acceptable for short clips; for long sequences you'd want to also slowly mutate the screen (or generate a fresh one and crossfade).
  • No wavelength dependence. Real heat shimmer is mildly chromatic (refractive index has weak dispersion), and at extreme scales you can see colour fringing on bright distant lights. We use a single achromatic warp.
  • Sky pixels get an arbitrary distance. A sky ray's atmospheric path is not really $f \cdot d_\max$; it's some integrated quantity that depends on viewing angle and atmospheric vertical structure. We use a single tunable factor as a controllable knob, not a derivation.
  • Per-pixel independent sampling. We re-look up each output pixel independently. A correct image-formation model would integrate the warp over the pixel's solid angle (anti-aliasing). Bilinear interpolation gives some of this for free; a more correct model would supersample.
